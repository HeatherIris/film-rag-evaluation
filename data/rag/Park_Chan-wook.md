# Park Chan-wook

## Basic Information

* Name: Park Chan-wook
* Date of Birth: August 23, 1963
* Nationality: South Korean
* Main Genres: Suspense, Thriller, Film Noir, Drama

## Overview of Directing Career

Born on 23 August 1963 in Seoul, South Korea, Park Chan-wook studied philosophy at Sogang University and participated in the photography and film club during his school years. His early works did not attract much attention, so he supported himself by writing film criticism and working as an assistant director.

He rose to fame in 2000 with ​Joint Security Area With the creative freedom that success brought, he launched his unofficial “Vengeance Trilogy”—Sympathy for Mr. Vengeance (2002), Oldboy (2003) and Lady Vengeance (2005)—establishing his strong personal style. Oldboy won the Grand Jury Prize at the 2004 Cannes Film Festival, marking his formal entry into the international film arena.

Since then, Park has balanced commercial appeal with artistic ambition: Thirst won the Cannes Jury Prize in 2009, Stoker was his English-language debut in 2013, and The Handmaiden earned BAFTA’s Best Non-English Language Film in 2016. His latest film, ​Decision to Leave​, won Best Director at Cannes in 2022.

He is known for exploring human darkness, moral ambiguity and emotional extremes within genre frameworks, and in recent years his style has shown a more introspective, mature side.

## Signature Stylistic Elements

### Visual Style and Photography

The film of Park Chan-wook has a distinct visual style characterized by his frequent use of wide-angle lenses, rapid zooms, push-pull shots, numerous close-ups of faces, and complex, fluid camera movements to create strong visual impact and psychological tension. His compositions are meticulously crafted, using space, lines, and geometric shapes to construct a sense of formal beauty.

Every scene and frame in Park Chan-wook’s films reflects his meticulous control and deliberate design. One particularly clever and precisely executed shot can be found in ​*Sympathy for Mr. Vengeance*​. The camera begins in the room next door to Ryu’s apartment, where four young men are masturbating, mistaking the sounds they hear for those of a woman in the throes of sexual pleasure. But as the camera slowly moves through their apartment and into Ryu’s, we see his sister writhing in pain on the floor, desperately trying to get the attention of her deaf-mute brother—who sits with his back to her, silently eating dinner, seemingly indifferent to her suffering. In this tracking shot, Park infuses the scene with dark humor and tragic irony through the jarring way these characters share the same cinematic space.

He makes bold and symbolically rich use of color, with each film having its own dominant color palette. Park is especially skilled at using visual texture to create a unique atmosphere. For example, *The Handmaiden* employs highly saturated colors to present a vivid, baroque-style aesthetic, while *Decision to Leave* features an overall blue tone, aligning with the film’s cold and intense emotional atmosphere.

Symbolic objects—such as octopuses, axes, shoes, pomegranates, and ants—and natural elements like mountains, the sea, snow, and cherry blossoms frequently appear in his films, serving as key carriers of narrative and thematic meaning.

Media like glass, mirrors, and screens are often used to create visual segmentation and layering. Through reflection and obstruction, these elements express character relationships, psychological states, or the blurred boundaries between reality and illusion.

### Narrative Structure

In terms of narrative, Park Chan-wook often employs non-linear storytelling and non-chapter structures, as seen in ​The Handmaiden. He disrupts the timeline and continually builds suspense through shifting character perspectives, then reveals the truth using flashbacks, interludes, and subjective viewpoints. He boldly experiments within genre conventions such as crime, thriller, revenge, and romance, breaking away from traditional narrative formulas. His plots are frequently accompanied by unexpected twists that challenge audience expectations, as exemplified in ​Oldboy​. The relationships between characters are filled with deception, testing, and disguise.

### Violence and Dark Humor

Park Chan-wook’s early and mid-career films are rich in dark humor and marked by the portrayal of violence and desire. Within scenes of extreme violence or tension, he often inserts absurd, bleak, or coldly humorous moments to balance the pacing of the film. He channels his characters’ anger and repressed frustration into terrifying acts of violence. The brutal elements of violence in his films resonate strongly with the aesthetics of Asian extreme cinema, while the depth of emotional texture elevates them to the level of Greek tragedy. The characters’ violent actions often stem from a compassionate love for others—an irony that brings with it an overwhelming sense of sorrow. However, this bold, stylized, and almost ritualistic approach becomes more restrained in ​Decision to Leave.

### Theme

Revenge is a central theme in many of Park Chan-wook’s films, most notably in his renowned “Vengeance Trilogy.” He is adept at exploring the motives and processes of revenge, the karmic cycles of fate that unfold between characters as a result, and the devastating consequences filled with sorrow and despair.

Characters in his films are often placed in extreme situations, operating within legal gray areas where moral boundaries are blurred. His narratives examine the choices people make when faced with such dire circumstances, revealing complex aspects of human nature.

At the same time, there are often deep divides between characters due to differences in identity, language, and hidden secrets. These gaps create a sense of constant surveillance and voyeurism, which in turn fuels misunderstanding and tension among characters.

Films don't shy away from the dark side of humanity. The people in Park’s films are driven by powerful desires and obsessions—whether for love, control, or revenge. These irrational forces often dictate their behavior. His works delve deeply into how such fixations shape the characters’ fates, with many ultimately propelled by external forces and internal flaws toward tragic ends.

On the other hand, Park’s films are also rich in criticism of patriarchal systems. In works like ​*The Handmaiden*​, he provides profound depictions of oppression within patriarchal societies and the spirit of female resistance and liberation.

## Directorial Philosophy

Park Chan-wook’s filmmaking philosophy is both profound and unique.

* **Feeling the Film:** Park hopes that viewers of his films do not remain solely at the intellectual or conceptual level. He wants the audience to physically and viscerally experience his work. He aims to stimulate all of the viewer’s senses, which is why he places particular emphasis on the construction of visual, auditory, and tactile details, striving to create an immersive and sensory-rich cinematic experience.
* **Reinventing Genre:** He does not adhere to traditional genre conventions and enjoys blending different genres—such as suspense, romance, and film noir. Within these frameworks, he explores deeper questions of human nature and philosophy. He often designs plot twists or endings that defy genre expectations, challenging viewers’ viewing habits and moral assumptions.
* **Exploring the Complexity and Darkness of Humanity:** Park’s films do not shy away from discomforting themes such as violence and desire. However, he emphasizes that these elements are not included for shock value or sensationalism, but rather to serve the in-depth exploration of characters’ extreme emotions, desires, and moral dilemmas. In interviews, he has stressed that when something so dark is depicted in such a beautiful way, it can ironically reveal and confront the complexity, ambiguity, and latent darkness of human nature and circumstances.
* **The Relationship Between Technology and Film History:** He believes that the development of film technology is closely intertwined with artistic expression. He actively embraces new techniques in cinematography and editing. Park was one of the earliest filmmakers in South Korea to use digital cameras, digital sound mixing, and digital color grading, and among the first in the world to shoot a short film using a smartphone.
* **The Power of Tragedy:** In telling stories about love or life, he often incorporates a sense of tragedy, believing that tragedy lends greater emotional power and depth to the narrative—as seen in *Decision to Leave* and its portrayal of tragic love. He is drawn to stories of “love that cannot be,” or separations caused by fate or by personal flaws.
* **Philosophy and Film:** Before becoming a filmmaker, Park studied philosophy. He originally intended to focus on philosophical aesthetics, but ended up attending Sogang University, which did not offer courses in aesthetics. Instead, as a Jesuit school, most of the curriculum was taught by priests and focused on medieval Catholic philosophy, which he found uninteresting. Not long after entering university, he became increasingly fascinated by film and photography, leading him to pursue a career in cinema. Today, Park does not follow any particular school of philosophy or specific philosopher. What he gained from studying philosophy was the approach of not merely addressing the surface of a topic, but following its logic all the way to the end.

## Key Influences:

* Alfred Hitchcock: Park Chan-wook has said that the film that made him want to become a filmmaker was Hitchcock’s ​Vertigo. When he first began studying cinema seriously, he was deeply influenced by Hitchcock. The legendary director remains one of Park’s major sources of inspiration. Park has studied Hitchcock’s techniques for building suspense, his methods of visual storytelling, his psychological depth in character portrayal, and his use of the theme of voyeurism.
* Greek Tragedy: Especially the works of Sophocles, which had a significant impact on Park’s ​*Vengeance Trilogy*​. These stories emphasize the inescapability of fate and the tragic destiny of characters driven toward destruction by forces beyond their control. ​Oldboy​, for instance, tells a bold revenge tale in which the protagonist ultimately becomes a tragic figure—much like King Lear, undone by his own folly.
* Manga and Cartoons: Early works such as Oldboy were adapted from Japanese manga. Some of Park’s exaggerated, stylized depictions of violence and dynamic visual effects are considered to be influenced by Japanese manga and animation.

## Key Collaborators

* ​Screenwriter Jeong Seo-kyung: A long-time collaborator of Park Chan-wook, co-writing several major works including ​Lady Vengeance, ​Thirst, ​The Handmaiden​, and ​Decision to Leave​.
* ​Composer Jo Yeong-wook​: Provided the musical scores for many of Park’s most important films, contributing to his signature cinematic sound.
