# Film noir

## Origins and Development

The term “Film Noir” was first coined by French film critic Nino Frank in 1946 to describe a wave of post–World War II Hollywood crime films that differed from traditional gangster pictures by emphasizing character psychology and social critique. The classic Film Noir era generally spans the 1940s through the 1950s. These films were deeply influenced by German Expressionist cinema’s visual experimentation, French Poetic Realism’s poetic depiction of everyday life in the 1930s, and American hard-boiled detective fiction by writers such as Dashiell Hammett and Raymond Chandler, which shaped themes of crime, guilt, and redemption. In addition, the traumas of World War II and the anxieties of the early Cold War imbued these films with a pervasive mistrust of authority and a critical view of the American “dream.”

## Aesthetic Characteristics

* ​Low-Key Lighting*: Employs strong contrasts of light and shadow to create a repressive, mysterious atmosphere, directly drawing on German Expressionism’s chiaroscuro techniques.
* ​Unconventional Composition​: Features Dutch angles, extreme close-ups, and frame-splitting through reflective surfaces to heighten characters’ psychological instability.
* ​Urban Nightscapes​: Focuses on nighttime city settings; streetlights, neon signs, fog, and rain all contribute to the sense of a lonely, unforgiving metropolis.
* ​Black-and-White Film Stock​: Most classic noirs were shot in black and white, enhancing the interplay of light and dark and the subtle gradations of gray.

## Common Narrative Themes

* ​Moral Ambiguity & Fatalism​: Protagonists—often ordinary people or private detectives—are reluctantly drawn into crime, face corruption and violence, and find their fates seemingly sealed.
* ​Femme Fatale​: Alluring but dangerous women who manipulate male protagonists with their desires, highlighting conflicts of gender and power.
* ​Social Critique​: Films often hint at postwar America’s inequalities, abuses of power, and the looming threat of the Cold War under the guise of crime narratives.
* ​Psychological Anxiety​: Reflections of wartime trauma, identity crises, and general distrust permeate the characters’ inner lives, mirroring broader societal anxieties.

## Visual Style

* ​High-Contrast Lighting​: Chiaroscuro effects leave half of a character’s face in shadow, symbolizing the struggle between light and dark within human nature.
* ​Deep Focus​: Utilizes deep-focus cinematography so that both foreground and background remain sharply in focus, creating complex, layered compositions.
* ​Reflections & Mirrors​: Recurring motifs of water, glass, and mirrors suggest themes of surveillance, split identities, and the elusiveness of truth.
* ​Grainy Film Texture​: Visible film grain, light specks, and scratches lend a weathered, world-weary quality to the image.

## Representative Films (10 Titles)

* The Maltese Falcon (1941)
  Director: John Huston
  Stars: Humphrey Bogart, Mary Astor, Peter Lorre, Sydney Greenstreet
  Synopsis: Various factions compete to obtain a jeweled falcon statuette. Private detective Sam Spade navigates betrayal and murder but ultimately upholds his professional integrity.
  Why It’s Noir: Establishes the noir template with its hard-boiled detective hero, femme fatale, moral ambiguity, and shadow-laden visuals.
* Double Indemnity (1944)
  Director: Billy Wilder
  Stars: Barbara Stanwyck, Fred MacMurray, Edward G. Robinson
  Synopsis: An insurance salesman conspires with a seductive housewife to stage her husband’s “accidental” death for the insurance payout. Their scheme unravels in mutual suspicion and police scrutiny, ending in tragedy.
  Why It’s Noir: Exemplifies the fatal attraction of the femme fatale, the inexorable pull of fate, and intense psychological tension.
* Laura (1944)
  Director: Otto Preminger
  Stars: Gene Tierney, Dana Andrews
  Synopsis: A detective becomes enamored with the portrait of the murdered Laura. As he investigates, he uncovers twists that challenge his assumptions about her fate.
  Why It’s Noir: Uses portraiture and psychological projection to explore obsession, desire, and the idealization of its female subject.
* Out of the Past (1947)
  Director: Jacques Tourneur
  Stars: Robert Mitchum, Jane Greer
  Synopsis: A former detective is drawn back into the criminal underworld by an old flame, leading to a web of deceit, escape, and pursuit.
  Why It’s Noir: Features rain-soaked streets, doomed romance, and a sense of inescapable past mistakes.
* Gilda (1946)
  Director: Charles Vidor
  Stars: Rita Hayworth, Glenn Ford
  Synopsis: A love triangle emerges between a casino owner, his loyal henchman, and the owner’s enigmatic wife, Gilda, whose loyalties remain uncertain.
  Why It’s Noir: Rita Hayworth’s portrayal of Gilda epitomizes the dangerous, sexualized femme fatale and the theme that beauty can be deadly.
* The Big Sleep (1946)
  Director: Howard Hawks
  Stars: Humphrey Bogart, Lauren Bacall
  Synopsis: Detective Philip Marlowe is hired to resolve a blackmail case in a wealthy family, but he soon finds himself amid murders, deceit, and gang conflict.
  Why It’s Noir: Combines witty, hard-boiled dialogue with nighttime cityscapes and an intricate, twisting plot.
* Sunset Boulevard (1950)
  Director: Billy Wilder
  Stars: William Holden, Gloria Swanson
  Synopsis: A struggling screenwriter becomes entangled with a faded silent-film star living in seclusion, leading to delusion and desperate acts.
  Why It’s Noir: A scathing critique of Hollywood dreams gone sour, using stark black-and-white cinematography to contrast illusion with decay.
* The Third Man (1949)
  Director: Carol Reed
  Stars: Orson Welles, Joseph Cotten
  Synopsis: In postwar Vienna, a writer searches for his friend Harry Lime, only to discover Lime’s involvement in a black-market drug ring.
  Why It’s Noir: Utilizes war-torn European backdrops, unconventional camera angles, and a haunting zither score to forge a uniquely continental noir.
* Touch of Evil (1958)
  Director: Orson Welles
  Stars: Orson Welles, Charlton Heston, Janet Leigh
  Synopsis: Following a car bombing on the U.S.–Mexico border, a Mexican detective and an American official clash over corruption and justice, culminating in a violent showdown.
  Why It’s Noir: Features a legendary single-take opening shot, complex moral corruption, and a brooding border-town setting as a bridge to neo-noir.
* The Postman Always Rings Twice (1946)
  Director: Tay Garnett
  Stars: Lana Turner, John Garfield
  Synopsis: A drifter takes a job at a roadside diner, falls for its unhappy wife, and they plot to murder her husband—only to be undone by their own passions and the law.
  Why It’s Noir: A potent mix of lust, betrayal, and murder that exemplifies noir’s fascination with illicit desire and fatal consequences.
